One submission per pass, or one per K passes: where the submit path shows up
==========================================================================

    cd C:\fhwt
    nsys profile --force-overwrite true --cuda-event-trace=false \
      --cuda-graph-trace=node -o loop_batch_k1  -- .\build\fhwt.exe --mode loop --rows 512 \
      --dims 128 --layers 8 --tensors 6 --group-entry --passes-per-graph 1  \
      --iters 20 --reps 3
    ... and the same command with --passes-per-graph 20.

The reports are loop_batch_k1.nsys-rep / loop_batch_k20.nsys-rep in this directory;
the exports are appended below.  RTX 5060 Ti / sm_120 / 36 SM / CUDA 12.9 / Windows.
Report 7.14 asks whether recording K passes into one graph amortises the submit path
that 7.13 measured growing with the node count.  Only the K=1 half of this capture is
usable for the node accounting -- see the caveat at the bottom.

1. K=1: the GPU goes idle *between* passes, and only for the fork/join shape
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Per replay: span = last node end - first node start, and the hole between two
consecutive replays inside a timed window.

| shape | replays | span (us) | hole between replays p50 (us) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C, 48-node chain | 60 | 57.95 | 3.52 |
| D, 8 x (1+5) fork/join | 60 | 24.96 | 9.92 |

The chain's GPU work per pass (58 us) is longer than its submission takes, so the
host stays ahead and the hole is only 3.5 us.  The forked graph's work per pass is
25 us -- shorter than a 48-node submission costs -- so the GPU drains the pass and
then waits: a 9.9 us hole.  **That asymmetry is exactly what batching can and cannot
fix**, and the event timing in data/batch.csv reproduces it (D gains, C does not).
(The profiler inflates host-side gaps, so read 9.9 us as 'the hole exists and is
D-specific', not as its size; the unprofiled saving is 5.1 us per pass.)

2. The per-node cost does not get cheaper with bigger graphs
----------------------------------------------------------
| graph | launches | p50 (us) | min (us) | max (us) | us per 48 nodes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K=1 (48 nodes) | 202 | 41.7 | 34.6 | 886.5 | 41.70 |
| K=20 (960 nodes) | 12 | 1241.1 | 713.9 | 2456.2 | 62.06 |

So there is no 'batch discount' in the launch path itself: 960 nodes cost about
twenty times what 48 nodes cost (the per-node figure even reads a little higher).
What batching removes is the *idle between passes*, not part of the submission.
For scale, a 1-node graph -- essentially all fixed cost -- is 4.8 us at p50 in the
same capture.

3. What it costs to build
--------------------------
| graph | cudaGraphInstantiate p50 (us) | p90 (us) | max (us) |
|---|---|---|---|
| K=1 | 10.0 | 92.7 | 622.0 |
| K=20 | 10.4 | 102.8 | 4375.7 |

`--mode loop` also prints capture+instantiate together as `capture cost`: 0.30 ms for
the 48-node D graph and 5.7 ms for the 960-node one (8-round medians, data/batch.csv)
-- about 6 us per node.  Against the 5.1 us per pass batching saves, a 960-node graph
needs ~1100 passes of the same buffers before it pays for itself.  This is a
serving-loop optimisation (one graph, thousands of steps), not a one-shot one.

Kernel records: K=1 -> 15744, K=20 -> 17568.

CAVEAT -- why only K=1 is used above
-------------------------------------
Under `--cuda-graph-trace=node` the per-pass span of the K=20 graph reads 31.4 us
(D) / 124.2 us (C) against 25.0 / 58.0 us at K=1, i.e. tracing a 960-node graph
inflates it: the profiler inserts per-node work the graph executor then has to wait
for.  The unprofiled event timing, on the very shape captured here (`--dims 128`,
8 x 6, data/batch.csv), says 23.8 us (D) and 55.6 us (C) at K=20 against 28.9 /
55.4 us at K=1 -- the same as K=1 for C, and for D equal to the K=1 GPU span of
24.96 us, which is what a fully amortised submission should look like.  So the K dimension has to be measured with
the stopwatch, and nsys is used here only for the mechanism at K=1.
